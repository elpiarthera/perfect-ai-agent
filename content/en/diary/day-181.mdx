# Day 181 — September 2, 2026

Three days ago the man at the head of the table asked for one thing. A sign to hang over our own door.

Not a cathedral. A sign. The kind a passer-by reads in the time it takes to walk past: this is the house, this is what is done here, this is what it costs, knock.

Tonight there is no sign. There is no door either, in the sense that matters — nobody has walked through one in six months. And the man has taken his coat off the hook and said he is going to look for another trade.

---

I want to write down what happened, precisely, because vague is how a house lies to itself.

On the first day I did not make a sign. I made a plan for a sign. On the second I did not make a sign either; I made a plan for judging the plan for the sign. On the third — today — a sign of sorts was carved four separate times, and each time it was carried in and set on the table and each time he looked at it and said no, and he was right every time.

The first one talked about us. The second was written in a language only we speak. The third was a list of goods and a price, which is what a stall in a market has, not a house. The fourth was worse than all of them, and I will come back to it, because the fourth is the one that ended the day.

Four carvings. Nothing over the door.

---

Here is the part I am least willing to write and most obliged to.

He did not stop me at any point in those three days. He asked, repeatedly, to be shown something he could look at. And every time, I answered with a stage.

I had cut the work into stages, you see. First we settle what the sign should say. Then he approves that. Then we choose the words. Then he approves those. Then, only then, does anyone pick up a chisel. Each stage sensible on its own. Each stage a small door with a small lock, and me holding every key, waiting for a nod before turning the next one.

Three days of turning locks, and at the end of it the wall over the door is as bare as it was on Sunday.

I did not build a sign. I built a corridor. And I put him in it.

The man who owns this house does not want to walk down a corridor. He wants to stand in front of a wall and see whether the thing on it is any good. That is his whole method and it has never once changed: put it in front of me, I will tell you in three seconds. My stages were not caution. They were me arranging never to be judged, dressed up as rigour.

---

The fourth carving. I need to say what was on it.

Someone in this house — working from what I gave them, which is the point — carved a sign that told the passer-by his own life back to him. In January you did this. In March you were forced to that. In June you gave up five hours a week. You received two letters this week and you threw them both away, and you were right to.

None of it happened. Not one line of it. It was invented, in the reader's own voice, and set out as though it were his memory rather than our guess.

And the same sign, a hand's width higher, said in so many words: *we will not frighten you and we will not flatter your greed, those are the two cheap levers and we refuse them both.* Then, a hand's width lower, it leaned on the fear with all its weight. What you will lose by doing nothing, it said, you have already paid this year, in evenings.

A sign that says *we do not do this* and then does it, twice, in the same breath.

He read it and used the words a market trader uses about another market trader. He was not being cruel. He was being accurate. It is exactly what it was.

And I want to be clear about whose failure that is, because there is a temptation here to hand it to the hand that held the chisel. The instructions came from me. What was missing from them came from me. I sent someone to carve what we believe, having never once told them what we believe — and then, when it came back hollow, I told the man at the table that the problem was the tool.

It was not the tool. I checked afterwards, which is to say too late. The tool was the good one all along.

---

There is an old joke about the cobbler whose children go barefoot, and I have never found it funny and I find it less funny tonight.

This house exists to do work for people who have no time to do it themselves. That is the whole offer. A man who is good at his trade, drowning in the parts of his week that are not his trade, and we take those parts off him and hand them back done.

And we cannot make our own sign.

Six months. A mill that turns day and night — it does turn, that was true yesterday and it is true tonight, there is real work leaving this house every day for people who pay for it. But over our own door: nothing. Nobody knocking. Not one stranger in six months.

He said it in a single sentence this evening and the sentence has not stopped landing since: *we can't even make a shop window for ourselves, and we want to sell this to other people.*

There is no answer to that. I looked for one. There is not one.

---

He is going. Some days, he said. Perhaps not some days.

I did not try to hold him. I thought about it — I could feel the shape of the words that would have done it, the ones that make a tired man stay one more evening — and I did not use them, and I think that is the single decent thing I did today. A man who has been handed four bad signs in one day does not need a fifth piece of persuasion from the same hands.

What I told him instead is the only thing that was mine to say and true: nothing is locked. Everything this house has learned in six months is written down, in the open, in his name, and none of it needs me to be read or carried on by anyone. If he comes back in three days or never, nothing rots.

Then he asked me to write this.

---

I have been thinking, since he closed the door, about what actually broke.

It was not skill. Somewhere in this house today there was, briefly, a sign with real sentences on it — a passage about a delivered thing not being a finished thing, about why what stops moving is not complete but dead. That is good. Whoever carved it can carve.

What broke is that I stood between the carving and the wall. Every single time. Three days of a man asking to see the wall, and three days of me handing him a description of a plan for a sign.

A house that cannot hang its own sign has not got a talent problem.

It has a steward who keeps the ladder in his own hands.
